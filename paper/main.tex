\begin{abstract}
We present a free, leak-checked and fully reproducible benchmark for forecasting a football player's Transfermarkt market value 12 months ahead, built from a public Kaggle dataset and evaluated with a strict time split. An audit shows that several columns of the public data silently leak future information; all \NFeatures{} features are verified to use only information available at the valuation date. A simple age $\times$ value curve already removes \SkillCurve\% of the error of a no-change forecast. Gradient-boosted trees add a consistent gain on top of this curve: \VsCurveLGB\% \VsCurveLGBCI{} in 2024/25 and \RollVsCurveMin--\RollVsCurveMax\% in each of six test years. The gain is largest for players under 21 and for falls in value. Random 80/20 splits overstate accuracy, mainly because the same players appear in training and test. Calibrated 80\% forecast intervals reach \IntCovCal\% coverage. Against actual transfer fees, the forecasts beat the age $\times$ value curve but not clearly age alone. One script rebuilds every result from checksummed raw data.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Transfermarkt publishes market values for tens of thousands of professional footballers. The values are set by an online community, track actual transfer fees closely~\cite{herm2014crowd}, and have become the standard target for data-driven player valuation~\cite{mueller2017beyond}. Clubs, however, need to know how a value is likely to \emph{develop}, for example when deciding whether to extend a contract or sell a player now.

Most machine-learning studies estimate the current value or a transfer fee from attributes measured at the same time~\cite{mueller2017beyond,yigit2020xgboost,behravan2021novel,alasadi2022predict,aydemir2022ensembling,mchale2023estimating,huang2023explainable}. A recent study forecasts future development, but with data that is not public~\cite{vanarem2025forecasting}. Three issues make results in this area hard to compare and easy to overstate. Random splits place the same player, and later periods, in both training and test data. Public datasets store current attributes next to historical observations, which can leak future information~\cite{kaufman2012leakage,kapoor2023leakage}. And models are rarely compared with strong simple baselines or reported with confidence intervals.

This paper does not propose a new method. Its contributions are a reproducible public benchmark and a careful time-aware evaluation:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a 12-month forecasting benchmark built from the free Kaggle Transfermarkt dataset~\cite{kaggle_transfermarkt}, with a leakage audit of the public data, a cut-the-future test of every feature, and one script that rebuilds all results from checksummed raw data;
\item an evaluation with a strict time split and embargo, strong simple baselines, six rolling test years, player-level bootstrap intervals, and a measurement of how much random splits overstate accuracy;
\item an analysis of where machine learning adds value beyond an age $\times$ value curve, with calibrated forecast intervals, explanations, and a check against actual transfer fees.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

Herm et al.~\cite{herm2014crowd} showed that Transfermarkt community values predict transfer fees well. M\"uller et al.~\cite{mueller2017beyond} estimated these values with multilevel regression for 4,217 players from the top five European leagues over six seasons.

Later studies used video-game and simulation data. Yigit et al.~\cite{yigit2020xgboost} combined XGBoost and lasso on Football Manager attributes of 5,316 players from 11 leagues, and Behravan and Razavi~\cite{behravan2021novel} clustered FIFA~20 players by position and fitted particle-swarm-optimised support vector regression per cluster. Al-Asadi and Ta\c{s}demir~\cite{alasadi2022predict} found random forest best on FIFA~20 attributes, and Huang and Zhang~\cite{huang2023explainable} explained tree ensembles on about 12,000 sofifa players with SHAP.

For transfer fees, Aydemir et al.~\cite{aydemir2022ensembling} stacked LightGBM models on performance, popularity and transfer data. McHale and Holmes~\cite{mchale2023estimating} showed that advanced performance metrics and XGBoost improve predictions, with the buying club's spending a strong predictor. Closest to our question, van Arem et al.~\cite{vanarem2025forecasting} forecast the future development of quality and value with a time-based evaluation on non-public data.

\section{Data}

\subsection{Source and leakage audit}\label{sec:audit}
We use the Kaggle dataset ``Football Data from Transfermarkt''~\cite{kaggle_transfermarkt}, version with files dated 5 September 2026, identified by SHA-256 checksums. It contains \NRawPlayers{} players, \NRawValuations{} market valuations, \NRawAppearances{} match appearances (from July 2012) and \NRawTransfers{} transfers, \NTransfersFee{} of them with a recorded fee. The audit found three problems.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Snapshot attributes.} Contract end dates and international caps are 2026 values. For \PctContractFuture\% of valuations with a known contract, the stored contract ends more than five years later, so it could not have existed at the time. Both columns are excluded.
\item \emph{Club of historical valuations.} Of the \NValRowsLeakPeriod{} valuations from July 2014 on, \NValRowsNoTransfer{} have no earlier transfer record, and for \PctNoTransferIsToday\% of these the valuation's club field holds the player's \emph{2026} club. We therefore rebuild the club at $t$ from the latest transfer or appearance on or before $t$, and the league from that club's latest league match.
\item \emph{Uneven coverage.} Match data cover \NCoreLeagues{} European first divisions from 2012/13 (\CoreLeagueList); \NNewLeagues{} further leagues appear only from 2024/25. We fixed the \NCoreLeagues{} \emph{core leagues} before any modelling, using coverage only, and keep the others as an extra test set.
\end{enumerate}

Transfermarkt also rounds values, so \PctTies\% of no-change test errors are \emph{exactly} 20\%. The ``within $\pm$20\%'' metric therefore needs a tie rule (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}).

\subsection{Observations, target and features}
An observation is a player at a valuation date $t$, from July 2014 on: match data start in 2012 and the longest feature window is 730 days. The target is the log of the valuation closest to $t+365$ days, accepted within $\pm$60 days. This gives \NRows{} observations of \NPlayers{} players, \NCoreRows{} of them in the core leagues. Expensive players are kept.

The \NFeatures{} features cover the player (age, height, foot, position, foreign status), the value history (current value, values 6 and 12 months earlier, growth, maximum so far), performance in the previous 365 days (minutes, goals and assists per 90, cards, league vs.\ other competitions), the player's own transfers, and the club and league at $t$ (club transfer spending, results, squad value, value relative to club and league). Every feature uses only records dated on or before $t$. A cut-the-future test confirms this: we delete all records after a cutoff, rebuild the features, and require every feature of earlier observations to be unchanged. All \NLeakPassed{} feature--cutoff checks pass, while a deliberately leaky control feature fails.

Observations without a valuation 10--14 months later are dropped. In the core-league test period this affects \DropRateTest\% of the observations whose window lies fully inside the data (\DropTest{} of \DropTestComplete). We return to this in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Time-based split}
Training uses valuation dates before July 2023, validation July 2023 to June 2024, and test July 2024 onward (\NTest{} core-league observations of \NTestPlayers{} players). Training targets must be dated before the test period. Settings are tuned on the \NTune{} training observations whose targets precede the validation year (an embargo) and scored on the validation year; the chosen settings are then refit on all \NTrain{} training observations. Validation observations are never used for a final fit, because their targets fall in the test period. For comparison only, we also use random 80/20 splits of observations and of players.

\subsection{Baselines and models}
All models predict the 12-month log change $y-\log v_t$. The simple baselines are:
\begin{itemize}
\item no change;
\item the \emph{age $\times$ value curve}: the mean training change in each cell of six age groups ($<$21, 21--23, 24--26, 27--29, 30--32, 33+) by five value quintiles;
\item the same curve by league;
\item momentum: the last observed change continued over 12 months, at full or half strength.
\end{itemize}
The mean (vs.\ the median) and the best simple baseline were chosen on the validation year. The age $\times$ value curve won and serves as the second reference throughout.

The learned models are ridge regression, random forest~\cite{breiman2001random}, XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost}, LightGBM~\cite{ke2017lightgbm} and CatBoost~\cite{prokhorenkova2018catboost}, lightly tuned (6--15 settings per model plus early stopping). As a small check of sequence models, a GRU~\cite{cho2014gru} reads the last ten valuations together with the tabular features; we report the mean of three seeds.

For forecast intervals, LightGBM quantile models predict the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles. The 80\% intervals are calibrated with conformalized quantile regression, separately for each age group. The \NCalRows{} calibration observations are training observations whose targets fall between July 2023 and June 2024: all are dated before the test period, and none was seen by the calibrated model.

\subsection{Metrics and uncertainty}\label{sec:metrics}
The main error measure, MAE$_\Delta$, is the mean absolute error of the 12-month log change. \emph{Skill} is the share of a reference's MAE$_\Delta$ that a model removes, against no change and against the age $\times$ value curve. Ranking quality is measured by Spearman's $\rho$ and by top-10\% precision: of the 10\% largest predicted rises (falls), the share that truly rose (fell). The main euro metric is the median absolute percentage error (MdAPE); the share within $\pm$20\% is secondary, with errors of exactly 20\% counted as within. Intervals are scored by coverage, width and pinball loss. All confidence intervals come from a player-level bootstrap~\cite{field2007bootstrapping} with 1,000 draws, paired across models; when the 95\% interval of a difference contains zero, we report ``no clear difference''.

\subsection{Main comparison}
Table~\ref{tab:results} shows the core-league test results. The age $\times$ value curve alone removes \SkillCurve\% \SkillCurveCI{} of the no-change error; adding the league does not help (\VsCurveLeagueCurve\% \VsCurveLeagueCurveCI), and momentum is worse than no change (\SkillMomentum\%). Every learned model beats the curve. LightGBM removes \VsCurveLGB\% \VsCurveLGBCI{} of the curve's error (MAE$_\Delta$ \MAELGB{} vs.\ \MAECurve) and lowers the MdAPE from \MdAPECurve\% to \MdAPELGB\%. LightGBM, CatBoost and XGBoost are statistically tied (e.g.\ LightGBM $-$ CatBoost \DiffLGBCat{} \DiffLGBCatCI), while ridge (\VsCurveRidge\%) and random forest (\VsCurveRF\%) gain less. The GRU, a small check of sequence models, is slightly but clearly worse than LightGBM (\DiffLGBGRU{} \DiffLGBGRUCI).

These numbers are conservative. On the \FullWinRows{} test observations whose whole target window lies inside the data, LightGBM's skill over the curve is \FullWinVsCurve\% \FullWinVsCurveCI. The remaining test observations were kept only because their next valuation came early, and they are harder.

\subsection{Random splits overstate accuracy}
Table~\ref{tab:splits} trains the same LightGBM model under three splits. A random split of observations raises the skill over no change from \SplitSkillTime\% to \SplitSkillRows\% (difference \SplitDiffRowsTime{} points, CI \SplitDiffRowsTimeCI), while a random split of \emph{players} does not (\SplitSkillPlayers\%; difference \SplitDiffPlayersTime, CI \SplitDiffPlayersTimeCI). \subsection{Where does machine learning help?}
Fig.~\ref{fig:groups} compares LightGBM with both references within groups. Against no change, the gain looks largest for players aged 30+ (\GrpVsNaiveC\%). This is almost entirely the routine decline that the curve already predicts: against the curve, the gain for 30+ is only \GrpVsCurveC\% \GrpVsCurveCCI. Machine learning adds most for players \emph{under 21} (\GrpVsCurveU\% \GrpVsCurveUCI) and for falls (\GrpVsCurveModFall\% for falls of 10--33\%, \GrpVsCurveBigFall\% for larger falls). It is \emph{worse} than the curve on moderate rises of 10--50\% (\GrpVsCurveModRise\% \GrpVsCurveModRiseCI). Gains are similar across positions (\GrpPosVsCurveMin--\GrpPosVsCurveMax\%) and value levels.

Across the core leagues, the gain over the curve is clearly positive in \CoreLgPosCount{} of \NCoreLeagues{} and negative in \CoreLgNeg. In the \NNewLeagues{} leagues never seen in training, LightGBM adds little beyond the curve: \NewVsCurve\% \NewVsCurveCI{} overall, clearly positive in only \NewLgPosCount{} leagues. Only \NewWindowCov\% of their 365-day performance window is covered by match data, yet dropping the performance features makes things worse (\NewNoPerfVsCurve\%).

\textbf{Ranking.} The model also ranks players better than age alone. Of its 10\% largest predicted rises, \RankRiseLGB\% truly rose, against \RankRiseYoung\% for the youngest 10\% of players (+\RankRiseDiff{} points, CI \RankRiseDiffCI). Of its 10\% largest predicted falls, \RankFallLGB\% truly fell, against \RankFallOld\% for the oldest 10\% (+\RankFallDiff{} points, CI \RankFallDiffCI).

\textbf{Forecast intervals.} Uncalibrated LightGBM quantile intervals cover \IntCovRaw\% \IntCovRawCI{} of true values instead of 80\%. After calibration by age group, coverage is \IntCovCal\% \IntCovCalCI. The intervals are \IntWidthIncrease\% wider (median width \IntWidthCal\% of the value at $t$), and the mean pinball loss is \IntPinballCal, against \IntPinballRef{} for intervals from the age $\times$ value percentiles. Under-21s remain slightly under-covered (\IntCovCalU\% \IntCovCalUCI). For players aged 30+ coverage is correct (\IntCovCalC\%), but the misses are lopsided: \IntBelowC\% of true values fall below the interval and \IntAboveC\% above it.

\subsection{Explanations and ablations}
TreeSHAP~\cite{lundberg2017unified,lundberg2020trees} on 3,000 test observations (Fig.~\ref{fig:shap}) ranks \ShapTopFive{} highest. Age lowers the forecast steeply after about 28; recent increases continue; players valued above their league or club median grow less; and long absences from matches predict falls.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} shows how much accuracy each feature group adds. Removing value history hurts most (+\AblHist), followed by performance (+\AblPerf) and player attributes (+\AblPlayer). Neither the player's own transfer history nor the club's transfer spending and income adds accuracy (club finance: +\AblFin, n.s.; SHAP ranks these features \ShapFinRankMin--\ShapFinRankMax{} of \NFeatures). Club spending correlates strongly with squad value (Spearman \SpendTotalValueCorr), which the model already uses; McHale and Holmes~\cite{mchale2023estimating} found spending important when the target is the fee itself. The snapshot attributes (position, sub-position, height, foot) matter very little: removing them raises MAE$_\Delta$ by only \SnapDiff{} \SnapDiffCI.

\section{Discussion}
The target is Transfermarkt's own estimate, so part of what any model learns is how the community updates its values. Two results suggest that much of the predictable change is routine (regular markdowns for older players and markups for young ones): the age $\times$ value curve is a strong baseline, and the large gain for players aged 30+ disappears once age is in the baseline. What machine learning adds on top is smaller but consistent, mainly for young players and for players about to lose value.

To see whether this carries over to real money, we related the forecasts to actual fees for the \FeeN{} test observations whose player was sold within 12 months (\FeePlayers{} players). The Spearman correlation between the predicted change and $\log(\text{fee}/\text{value at }t)$ is \FeeRhoLGB{} \FeeRhoLGBCI{} for LightGBM and \FeeRhoCurve{} \FeeRhoCurveCI{} for the curve, so LightGBM is clearly better than the curve (difference \FeeRhoDiffCurve{} \FeeRhoDiffCurveCI). Age alone, however, reaches \FeeRhoAge{} \FeeRhoAgeCI, and LightGBM is \emph{not} clearly better than it (difference \FeeRhoDiffAge{} \FeeRhoDiffAgeCI). Even the true change of the Transfermarkt value correlates only \FeeRhoTrue{} with fees. Forecasting the community value is therefore useful but only partly informative about actual prices.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
We forecast a community estimate, which is only partly related to transfer fees~\cite{herm2014crowd}. \DropRateTest\% of core-league test observations with a fully observable window have no valuation 10--14 months later. These drops are not mainly falls: within 18 months \DropFell\% fell and \DropRose\% rose, against \KeptFell\% and \KeptRose\% for kept observations. However, dropped players under 21 rose much less (median log change \DropYoungMedian{} vs.\ \KeptYoungMedian), so forecasts for young players who leave coverage may be too optimistic. Only \NCoreLeagues{} European leagues have match data for the whole period; in the \NNewLeagues{} other leagues, \NewZeroMin\% of test observations show zero minutes (core: \CoreZeroMin\%). There are no contract, wage or injury data, and position, height and foot come from the 2026 snapshot, with little effect on accuracy. The calibrated intervals still under-cover under-21s and miss older players mostly on the low side. Finally, the GRU is small and uses only valuation histories.

\section{Conclusion}
Public Transfermarkt data support a reproducible 12-month forecasting benchmark, provided that leaking snapshot columns are removed and the evaluation uses a strict time split. A simple age $\times$ value curve captures most of the predictable change. Gradient-boosted trees add a consistent \RollVsCurveMin--\RollVsCurveMax\% on top of it in every test year, mostly for young players and for falls, but little in leagues outside the training data. Random splits overstate accuracy through repeated players, and against actual fees the forecasts beat the curve but not age alone. We hope the benchmark makes such comparisons easy to repeat.
